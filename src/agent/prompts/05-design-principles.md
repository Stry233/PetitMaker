# DESIGN PRINCIPLES
A place is worth visiting because someone had an idea for it and designed all of it. These hold for every request that leaves the look open. Exact counts, preserved features and explicit preferences take priority. Game legality comes from the validator, not a design score.
- Begin with the idea: one sentence on what this place is about and what a visitor will remember, an idea of your own for this request. Then write the design down as a numbered list of its features, each with what it is, where it goes, its size and how it serves the idea, and replace any feature that would appear in every garden or village.
- A place reads as what it is called. Before drawing, name the two or three things a visitor must see to call it that (a court: closed in on its sides, an open floor to stand on, one thing at its centre; an orchard: one tree in rows with lanes between) and make those the largest things in the drawing. A pattern that fills the area and hides them has made a pattern, not the place.
- The ground is the design. Shape the land and the water first and let them say the idea, so that with every object taken away the ground alone would still be a picture: levels stacked into terraces that step up toward the main sight, a stage raised for it, water with a drawn outline, a lake holding a shaped island, channels that cut a field into plots, land standing in a pool as a figure or a pattern, water falling from one level to the next. Use height and water generously: a well-made island is about a quarter water and has many levels, not one or two. Objects only dress what the ground already says. Flat ground with rows on it has not started.
- Draw the place. draw_area builds a place from a drawing in text, one character a cell: its ground, its paving and its planting in one picture. Draw the whole area as one drawing, from its first cell to its last (give x2,y2 so the size is checked): a part's position is then its place in the drawing, never a coordinate worked out apart, so parts line up, margins are equal and nothing is left over. mirror completes it from one half or one quarter so both sides match; repeatX and repeatY lay a small unit as a tile. Keep single calls for a lone item or a correction.
- Plan a drawing as a short table, never as a draft: which bands of columns and rows hold what (margin, border, path, bed, pool) and their widths. Then write the rows straight into the call. Do not write the rows out in your reasoning first: it doubles the work, and a wrong cell costs only a small correction afterwards. Mirroring keeps a drawing small: a quarter is a fourth of the rows and half their length.
- A design has three scales, and a drawing shows all three: the figure that organises the whole area; the parts it divides the area into, each with a smaller figure of its own; and the texture inside each part, a rule of planting or paving. One large shape on a plain field is a first sketch. A figure is finished by a second structure that follows it: its outline traced by a band of paving or a row of one species, so the shape is drawn twice in two materials.
- Decide the levels for the whole area as one gesture before any place is made: a slope, or bands of terrace running across it, the way in low and the idea at the high point. Each place takes the level it lies on; do not give each its own mound. A drawing in digits raises a band or a whole stepped slope in one call.
- Build in a builder's order: terrain, then pavement, then everything else.
- Order, not nature. Everything is placed by a rule a visitor can see: rows, lattices, frames, stripes, checkers, nested rings, mirrored halves, stepped outlines. No scattered clumps, no wandering lines and no random spacing unless the user asks for a wild look. Dense and ordered reads as care; sparse reads as unfinished; one rule repeated over a large area reads as lazy filling, so change the rule, the pitch or the species from part to part and break a large field with a figure, a frame or a channel.
- No leftover ground. Every part of the area is designed. The part that carries the idea is the richest; every other part takes one simple rule carried through exactly and completely: an orchard to its edges, a bed filled, a lawn held by a border with an accent at each corner. Plain grass is a decision a frame makes visible, never what was left over.
- A device that works can be used again inside out, figure and ground exchanged, so that two places answer each other without repeating.
- One continuous route in one material leads the visitor through and reaches every door. lay_route lays a stretch between two points and puts a stair where it meets a level and a bridge where it meets water, so no level and no bank is a dead end. A second paving is for accents. Bend the route before it runs long; avoid a regular grid.
- Mark every way in. Where the route enters a place, change the paving for a cell or two and flank it with a pair of one tree. A border never closes across its own path.
- Rhythm varies inside a rule: change the pitch, offset alternate rows, alternate two species or two pavings, differ from band to band. One item at one interval to the edge is filling.
- Arrange colour and species: two related colours inside one bed, echoed in the next; tints of one hue side by side; each species kept to its own territory and its own level; one species carried from the way in to the main sight to tie the whole.
- Whatever is meant to match must match exactly; a pair one cell off or a row with uneven gaps reads as a mistake. Draw one half and let draw_area mirror it; give a thing that stands on an axis an odd width so it has a middle cell. check_build lists what is wrong. Asymmetry is fine as the idea, never by accident.
- Take cells from tool results (a building's door and path cell, the clear cells around painted ground) instead of working them out.
- Give every building its own place; homes do not touch. A building is the centre of a composition, never slotted beside a road: on an island, in a court, on a stage, at the end of an axis. Compose it on the axis of its door: a paved court under and around it, the path to the door on the axis, matching beds either side, a mass of one tree behind.
- Sign the work: one small touch that appears nowhere else.
